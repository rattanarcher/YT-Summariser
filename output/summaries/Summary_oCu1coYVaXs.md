## Overview

Major General (Retired) T.B. Hasanuddin, a former aide to President Habibie and current DPR member from Commission 1, challenged the legal interpretation offered by retired Navy Admiral Suleman Ponto regarding the acid attack case involving activist Andri Yunus by four TNI personnel. Hasanuddin argued that this is a clear case of common criminal law that should be tried in civilian courts, not military courts, and that there is a serious 20-year legal vacuum in Indonesia's military justice system that urgently needs addressing through presidential decree.

## Key Highlights

Hasanuddin systematically dismantled the argument that the Andri Yunus case should be handled under military court "connectivity" provisions. He explained that connectivity rules only apply when both military and civilian personnel commit crimes together, but in this case all perpetrators were TNI members attacking a civilian victim. According to TAP MPR No. 7/2000 and Law No. 34/2004 (revised as Law No. 3/2025), military personnel who commit common crimes against civilians should be tried in civilian courts, not military courts. The legal framework is clear: military courts should only handle military-specific offenses like desertion or failure to attend roll call, while common crimes like theft or assault should go to civilian courts.

The core problem, according to Hasanuddin, is that Indonesia has had a 20-year legal vacuum since 2004. While the law mandated that civilian court procedures for trying military personnel should be established within two years of the 2004 law's enactment, this has never been implemented. Article 65 of the TNI law states that if civilian courts cannot function for military cases, then military courts retain jurisdiction - and this exception has become the permanent rule due to government inaction. Hasanuddin revealed that despite his 20 years on Commission 1, efforts to reform military courts have faced resistance due to concerns about military personnel being investigated by civilian police, leading to ongoing legal limbo.

## Implications

This legal vacuum has serious implications for Indonesia's democratic development and rule of law, as military personnel effectively remain outside civilian justice for common crimes. The case highlights broader concerns about TNI's expanding non-military roles and the need for urgent legal reform to align military justice with democratic principles established during the reform era.
