## Overview

This interview on Bocor Alus Politik features filmmaker Dhani Laksono, director of the controversial documentary "Pesta Babi" (Pig Party), discussing widespread censorship and intimidation surrounding his film about Papua. The documentary examines environmental destruction, land grabbing, militarism, and human rights violations in Indonesia's easternmost province. With 28 confirmed screening bans and thousands of screening requests, the film has become a lightning rod for debates about press freedom and Papua policy under President Prabowo's administration.

## Key Highlights

The documentary took three years to produce through grassroots collaboration, covering what Laksono describes as three major crises converging in Papua: the world's largest deforestation project spanning 2.5 million hectares, 107,000 internally displaced persons, and ongoing armed conflict with daily casualties. Despite Papua's problems being larger in scale than those previously seen in Aceh, Laksono argues they receive minimal media coverage due to deliberate internet shutdowns, high reporting costs, low audience engagement with Papua stories, and significant political risks for media outlets.

Laksono strongly refutes allegations that foreign funding drives the film's viral success, pointing to the organic nature of 4,000 screening requests from diverse groups including religious organizations, schools, cafes, and disability advocacy groups. He argues that if foreign money could orchestrate such grassroots momentum, other well-funded entities should be able to replicate this model, yet no one has successfully done so since he pioneered community screenings in 2014. The filmmaker sees Papua as Indonesia's "last frontier" both environmentally and as a source of legitimacy for renewed militarism, where the military gains public support specifically when operating against Papuan independence groups, despite controversial behavior elsewhere.

The film's controversial title was deliberately chosen over alternatives like "Red Cross" to avoid religious sensitivities while highlighting what Laksono calls Indonesia's misplaced priorities - being more offended by references to pigs than by corruption or violence. He views the widespread screening bans as unprecedented in Indonesia's 28-year reform era, representing a troubling shift toward increased authoritarianism that combines military power with oligarchic interests.

## Implications

The documentary and subsequent crackdowns signal escalating tensions over Papua policy under Prabowo's administration, potentially marking a return to more authoritarian governance styles. The film's viral success despite systematic suppression suggests deep public frustration with government transparency and environmental policies, while the militarized response to cultural screenings indicates the strategic importance of controlling Papua narratives for maintaining political legitimacy.
