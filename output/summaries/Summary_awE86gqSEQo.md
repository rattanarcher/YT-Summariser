# Overview

This video features former Indonesian Foreign Minister Hasan Wirajuda (2001-2009) being interviewed by Akbar Faizal about the diplomatic controversy sparked by Finance Minister Sri Mulyani's suggestion to impose tariffs on ships using the Malacca Strait. Wirajuda provides a detailed legal and diplomatic analysis of Indonesia's maritime sovereignty rights, explaining why the proposal violated international law and damaged Indonesia's diplomatic relationships. The discussion expands to cover broader issues of Indonesia's foreign policy under President Prabowo, including concerns about abandoning ASEAN as the anchor of Indonesian diplomacy and the need for stronger coordination mechanisms in foreign policy formulation.

# Key Highlights

Wirajuda begins by explaining the fundamental legal distinction that many Indonesian officials misunderstand regarding maritime sovereignty. In the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) areas of the Malacca Strait, Indonesia has sovereign rights, not full sovereignty - specifically rights to exploit natural resources like fishing, but not the authority to control international transit. Under the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, the Malacca Strait operates under a transit passage regime, guaranteeing foreign vessels continuous, expeditious passage without interference. The Finance Minister's tariff proposal violated this international legal framework because it would require ships to stop and pay fees, contradicting the principle of unimpeded transit that the global community relies upon for maritime commerce.

The former Foreign Minister draws parallels to a previous diplomatic crisis in 2006 when Singapore proposed allowing US special forces to secure the Malacca Strait against supposed terrorist threats. Wirajuda rejected this proposal, arguing that the security issues were actually sea piracy by unemployed fishermen during the economic crisis, not terrorism. He successfully coordinated with Malaysia to establish joint patrols by coastal states rather than allowing external military intervention. This example illustrates Indonesia's consistent position that coastal states - not user states like Singapore - have the authority to regulate strait security, while still respecting international transit rights.

Regarding President Prabowo's foreign policy approach, Wirajuda expresses concern about the lack of coherence and the apparent neglect of ASEAN as Indonesia's diplomatic anchor. While praising Prabowo's multi-alignment strategy of engaging with multiple global powers, he warns against losing focus on regional priorities. Wirajuda emphasizes that ASEAN and the East Asia Summit framework should remain central to Indonesian diplomacy, particularly as the region has become the world's economic center with four of the six largest economies by purchasing power parity. He calls for strengthening Prabowo's foreign policy team, noting the absence of a presidential advisory team and suggesting the need for experienced senior advisors.

# Implications

This analysis reveals significant gaps in institutional coordination and legal understanding within the Indonesian government that could damage the country's international relationships. The controversy highlights the critical need for better inter-ministerial coordination on foreign policy matters and deeper understanding of international maritime law among senior officials to prevent similar diplomatic embarrassments in the future.
