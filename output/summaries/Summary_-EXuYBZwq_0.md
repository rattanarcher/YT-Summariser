## Overview

Professor Rhenald Kasali hosted a discussion with Anton Supit, Vice President and Director of PT Toyota Motor Manufacturing Indonesia and Chairman of Labor Affairs at APINDO (Indonesian Employers' Association), examining the disconnect between Indonesia's official economic statistics and the reality facing businesses. While the government reports 5.39% economic growth and maintains fuel subsidies amid rising global oil prices, manufacturers are struggling with material shortages, rising costs, and employment pressures. The conversation highlighted fundamental structural issues in Indonesia's economy, particularly the high logistics costs, regulatory inefficiencies, and the challenge of creating sufficient formal employment opportunities.

## Key Highlights

The discussion revealed a stark contrast between what Supit called "two worlds" - the world of official discourse and seminars versus ground reality. While Indonesia's fiscal position is presented as strong with economic growth above 5%, manufacturers face severe challenges including shortages of critical materials like plastic, sulfur, aluminum, and magnetic components due to Middle East conflicts disrupting supply chains. These shortages, combined with insurance companies refusing to cover ships transiting conflict zones, are forcing companies to reduce operations and consider layoffs. The youth unemployment rate has reached 17-20%, with over 3.5 million new job seekers entering the market annually while economic absorption capacity remains limited at only 400,000 jobs per percentage point of growth in labor-intensive sectors.

A major concern is Indonesia's retreat toward command economy policies, reversing market-oriented reforms. The government now requires state-owned enterprises to handle imports of commodities like sugar, salt, and automotive components, creating inefficiencies and higher costs that ultimately burden consumers. Regional governments, facing budget cuts of over 30% in transfer funds (around 200 trillion rupiah reduction), are desperately raising local taxes and fees, potentially stifling economic growth. Supit argued that Indonesia's logistics costs remain among the world's highest at 6-7% of GDP compared to 3-4% in other ASEAN countries, largely due to bureaucratic inefficiencies and inconsistent policies across government agencies.

## Implications

This analysis suggests Indonesia faces a critical juncture where official economic optimism masks underlying structural weaknesses that could undermine long-term growth and employment creation. The combination of energy subsidies for all income levels, increased state control over trade, and regional fiscal stress creates an unsustainable economic model that may require significant policy reforms to address manufacturing competitiveness and job creation challenges.
